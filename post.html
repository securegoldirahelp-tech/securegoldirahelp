Inflation & Your IRA

What Inflation Is Quietly Doing to Your IRA (And What You Can Do About It)

IRA/401k Gold Retirement Guide  ·  Education

Imagine you did everything right. You saved faithfully for decades, built up a solid IRA or 401(k), and retired with a number that felt comfortable. Then, a few years later, you notice something strange — the balance looks about the same, but your money doesn't seem to go as far. Groceries cost more. Insurance went up. A dinner out that used to be $40 is now $65. The account hasn't shrunk, but somehow you feel poorer.

That feeling has a name. It's inflation. And for retirees living on savings, it's one of the most misunderstood risks out there.

Why Your Account Balance Can Look Fine While Your Purchasing Power Shrinks

Here's the thing most people don't realize: inflation doesn't show up in your account balance. Your IRA can sit at $300,000 year after year while your actual purchasing power quietly erodes.

Think of it this way. If inflation runs at 3% a year — roughly the historical U.S. average — then $300,000 today will only buy what $222,000 buys in 10 years. Your account didn't lose a dollar. But you lost about a quarter of your real wealth.

Now run that at 5% or 6%, which is what millions of retirees experienced in 2021–2023. The math gets uncomfortable fast.

Traditional IRAs and 401(k)s are typically invested in stocks, bonds, and mutual funds. These can grow — sometimes impressively — but they're all priced and measured in dollars. When the dollar loses purchasing power, so does everything denominated in it.

What Makes Inflation Especially Hard on Retirees

Younger investors have time and a paycheck. If inflation spikes and their portfolio takes a hit, they keep contributing and wait it out. Retirees are working from a different playbook entirely.

You're drawing from savings, not adding to them. That means:

  • Sequence of returns matters more. A bad stretch early in retirement, combined with withdrawals, can permanently reduce how long your money lasts.
  • Fixed income is hit hardest. Bonds and money market funds often fail to keep pace with inflation. The "safe" portion of your portfolio may actually be the most exposed.
  • Social Security only partially adjusts. Cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs) exist, but they've historically lagged real-world price increases in things like healthcare — which tends to rise faster than general inflation.

None of this means your IRA is in crisis. It means inflation deserves a place in your retirement thinking alongside market risk and longevity risk.

What Is a Gold IRA, Exactly?

You may have heard about gold IRAs in passing — maybe from a commercial, a friend, or an article. Here's what one actually is, without the sales pitch.

A gold IRA is simply a type of Individual Retirement Account that holds physical precious metals — gold, silver, platinum, or palladium — instead of (or alongside) stocks and bonds. It works the same way your existing IRA does in terms of tax treatment. The difference is in what's sitting inside it.

The gold is real, physical metal stored at an IRS-approved secure depository on your behalf. You don't take possession of it yourself — that's a key IRS rule — but it's yours.

Why do some retirees choose this? Gold has historically held its purchasing power over long periods of time. Unlike a dollar, you can't print more of it. It doesn't depend on the creditworthiness of a government or corporation. During periods of currency debasement or economic stress, it has often moved in the opposite direction from paper assets.

It's not a magic bullet. Gold doesn't pay dividends, and its price can swing in the short term. But as a partial hedge against inflation and currency risk, it's the option many retirement-focused investors turn to when they want something that doesn't move with the dollar.

Common Questions

Can I move my existing IRA into a gold IRA?

Yes, through a process called a rollover or a direct transfer. Your existing IRA funds move — either directly from custodian to custodian, or via a check you deposit within 60 days — into a new self-directed IRA that's set up to hold precious metals. Done correctly, there are no taxes or penalties on the transfer. Most reputable gold IRA providers walk you through the process step by step.

How much of my retirement savings should be in gold?

There's no single right answer, and any response that says otherwise should raise a flag. Many financial advisors who recommend gold at all suggest somewhere between 5% and 20% of a portfolio, depending on your age, risk tolerance, and overall financial picture. The idea is diversification — not abandonment of your existing accounts, but a meaningful allocation that moves differently than your stocks and bonds.

Is a gold IRA safe?

The IRS has strict rules about what gold qualifies (it must meet purity standards) and where it's stored (only at approved depositories). A gold IRA from a reputable custodian is a regulated financial account — not some gray-market scheme. That said, as with anything in personal finance, the quality of the company you work with matters. Look for established custodians, transparent fees, and no high-pressure sales tactics.

What are the fees involved?

Gold IRAs do carry fees that traditional IRAs often don't — typically a setup fee, annual storage and custodial fees, and sometimes a transaction fee when you buy or sell metals. These vary by provider. It's worth asking for a full fee schedule upfront and comparing two or three providers before you decide anything.

So What Should You Do?

There's no universal answer here. A gold IRA isn't the right move for everyone — it depends on your current accounts, your timeline, your other income sources, and how you feel about the direction of the dollar.

What is worth doing is understanding your options before inflation makes the decision for you.
